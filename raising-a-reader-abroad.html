Raising a Reader Abroad: Tips for Expat & Bilingual Families

✍️ By Sydney 📅 April 2026 ⏱️ 8 min read

You're raising your child somewhere English isn't the language of the street, the school, or the playground. Maybe you moved your family across an ocean. Maybe you've always lived here — and English is simply the language of your home, your heritage, or the future you're building for your kids. Either way, somewhere in the middle of all that, you're wondering: how do I make sure my child actually learns to read in English?

This is one of the most common concerns I hear from the families I work with — expats, worldschoolers, and bilingual families who speak English fluently at home but are raising children in countries where English simply isn't in the air. And I want to start with this: you are not behind, and your child is not behind. Whether you've moved your family across the world or you've always lived where you live and English is just the language of your household — giving your child English literacy is a gift. And with the right approach, it's absolutely achievable.

I'm Sydney, a certified reading interventionist with a Master's in Early Childhood Education from Johns Hopkins University. I've taught children to read in classrooms, in intervention settings, and in other countries — and I've taught my own kids to read while living abroad. So let me share what I know works.

Why English Literacy Feels Harder Outside an English-Speaking Country (And Why It's Not)

Expat parents often worry that because their child is immersed in a different language environment — hearing it at school, on the playground, at the shops — English literacy will suffer. Bilingual families have a related but distinct concern: English is present at home, but nowhere else. No ambient reinforcement, no English-speaking peers, no street signs or overheard conversations to back it up. Both of these worries are understandable. But they're rooted in a misunderstanding of how children actually learn to read.

Reading is not a natural skill. Unlike spoken language, which children absorb effortlessly through exposure, reading has to be explicitly taught. This is true whether you live in Ohio or the Netherlands, in Singapore or Vietnam. What matters most isn't the ambient linguistic environment — it's the quality and consistency of direct instruction your child receives in English literacy specifically.

The good news? That instruction can happen at home. It can happen in a 30-minute session a few times a week. And it can happen with a qualified teacher, even online, from anywhere in the world.

Bilingualism doesn't slow down reading development — research consistently shows that strong literacy in one language actually supports language learning across the board.

— Grounded in the Science of Reading research literature

5 Practical Tips for Raising a Reader Abroad

01

Make English Reading Part of Your Daily Rhythm

Consistency beats intensity every time. You don't need hour-long study sessions — you need 15 to 20 minutes of focused reading practice, most days of the week. Tie it to something that already exists in your routine: after breakfast, before bath time, or right after the school pickup.

Young children thrive on predictability. When reading happens at the same time in the same cozy spot, it stops feeling like a task and starts feeling like part of life. That's the goal.

02

Prioritize Phonics — and Don't Let Anyone Talk You Out of It

The Science of Reading is clear: structured phonics instruction is the foundation of fluent reading. That means your child needs to learn how letters map to sounds in a deliberate, sequential way — not through guessing from pictures, not through memorizing whole words by sight alone.

If your child's school uses a different reading approach — or is teaching reading in an entirely different language — it's worth supplementing with structured English phonics at home. This is true for expat families whose kids attend local schools, and equally true for bilingual families where English reading simply isn't happening anywhere else. A qualified reading tutor can provide consistent, evidence-based instruction in the method that works.

03

Build an English Book Environment at Home

Surround your child with English books — at their level, above their level, and well below their level. Books that are "too easy" build fluency and confidence. Books that are "too hard" (read aloud by you) build vocabulary and a love of stories. Decodable readers for practice, picture books for pleasure, and audiobooks while driving.

A well-stocked English bookshelf doesn't require a physical bookstore in your new city. Kindle, Epic!, Audible, and services like Beanstack or your local international school library can all help. Make it a cozy, inviting corner and your child will be drawn to it.

04

Embrace the Multilingual Superpower — Without Guilt

If your child is mixing languages, switching between English and the local language mid-sentence, or seems to be "behind" in English compared to peers back home — take a breath. This is developmentally normal for children in multilingual environments, and it is not a warning sign. This goes for expat kids and for bilingual children being raised with English at home in a non-English-speaking country.

What looks like confusion is actually cognitive flexibility. Research consistently shows that multilingual children develop stronger executive function, better metalinguistic awareness, and often catch up to — and surpass — monolingual peers in reading once formal instruction is consistent. Give your child (and yourself) grace here.

05

Get Support That Travels With You — or Stays Consistent at Home

For expat families, one of the biggest challenges is that your support network changes every time you move. The excellent reading tutor you found in Singapore stays in Singapore when you move to Lisbon. For bilingual families who aren't moving around, the challenge is different: finding someone qualified to teach English reading specifically, outside a school setting, is genuinely hard.

Online tutoring solves both problems. A good online reading specialist can follow an expat family from city to city, and provide bilingual families with expert English literacy instruction regardless of where in the world they live. You're not starting over, and you're not going it alone — you're building on a consistent foundation with qualified support.

What to Look for in a Reading Tutor for Your Child

Not all literacy support is created equal. Whether you're an expat family or a bilingual family raising English readers at home, here's what I'd recommend prioritizing in a tutor:

📋 Your Tutor Checklist

  • ✓Formal qualifications in early childhood education or reading intervention (not just teaching experience)
  • ✓Knowledge of the Science of Reading and structured phonics methodology
  • ✓Experience with young children ages 4–8 specifically — this age range requires a very particular skill set
  • ✓Genuine warmth and playfulness — a reluctant reader needs a tutor who makes sessions feel like fun, not school
  • ✓Clear communication with parents about progress, methods, and next steps
  • ✓Flexibility around time zones and scheduling
  • ✓A structured but adaptive approach — not a one-size-fits-all curriculum

A Note for Homeschooling Families — and Bilingual Families Going It Alone

Many of the families I work with are homeschooling or worldschooling — choosing to educate their children through travel and experience, often without a fixed curriculum. Others are bilingual families where English is the language of home, but no school, tutor, or program has ever formally taught their child to read in English. In both cases, the parent is carrying a lot.

I want you to know: you can absolutely do this, and doing it alongside a qualified specialist will give you confidence that the foundation is solid. Bringing in expert support for literacy specifically isn't giving up — it's one of the smartest investments you can make in your child's education. A specialist handles the formal instruction while you continue doing all the things only you can do: the conversations, the books at bedtime, the connection to your language and your family's story.

You don't have to figure this out alone.

The best thing you can do for your expat child's literacy isn't to recreate a classroom abroad. It's to make reading feel safe, joyful, and worth doing — and then make sure the instruction is solid underneath that joy.

— Sydney, Reading with Sydney

The Bottom Line

Raising a reader in English — outside an English-speaking country — is absolutely possible. Expat families do it. Bilingual families do it. Worldschooling families do it. And their kids grow into fluent, confident English readers, even while they're also learning Spanish, Dutch, Vietnamese, or Japanese at the same time.

What it takes is: consistent structured phonics instruction, a rich English reading environment at home, a patient and playful approach, and — ideally — qualified support you can trust.

If you're wondering where to start, the most important step is simply starting. Even 15 minutes a day of focused phonics practice, four days a week, will make a measurable difference within months.
